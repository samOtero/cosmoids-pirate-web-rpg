# Cosmoids Story, Themes, and Dialogue Guide

This document is the narrative source of truth for the game's premise, world, tone, themes, and dialogue style. New story content should remain consistent with these guidelines.

## High Concept

Cosmoids is a web-based, creature-collecting tower defense adventure set in a colorful pirate era. Players sail across a vast ocean, visit different islands, battle and capture wild or enemy Cosmoids, and add those creatures to their party as towers.

The core progression includes:

- Capturing enemy Cosmoids and adding them to the player's party.
- Leveling up and evolving Cosmoids.
- Learning new attacks.
- Exploring islands and meeting new people, factions, and creatures.
- Following an ongoing story that gradually reveals the truth behind the Stone Curse.

## Tone and Creative Touchstones

The tone is a lighthearted, all-ages adventure inspired by the sense of discovery, warmth, humor, and momentum found in *Pokémon*, *The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker*, and *One Piece*.

The story may use the broad rhythm of early *One Piece* arcs: arrive at a distinctive island, meet its people, uncover a local injustice or mystery, confront the force behind it, and leave with a new bond or clue. It must not reproduce existing characters, locations, dialogue, signature scenes, or plotlines one-to-one. Cosmoids should have its own mythology, conflicts, character relationships, and resolutions.

Adventure and comedy should remain at the surface, even as the larger story introduces danger, grief, corruption, and cruelty. Dark moments should create empathy and urgency without making the world feel hopeless or grim.

## Core Themes

- **Freedom and dreams versus control:** Honorable adventurers chase personal dreams and the freedom to choose their own course, while tyrants and corrupt authorities try to control people through fear.
- **Companionship versus exploitation:** A Cosmoid is a partner and living creature, not merely a weapon. How a person treats Cosmoids reveals their character.
- **Courage versus fear:** The Stone Curse causes understandable fear, but panic and prejudice can become as dangerous as the curse itself.
- **Curiosity versus rigid certainty:** Exploration, compassion, and a willingness to question accepted beliefs are necessary to discover the cure.
- **Institutions are made of people:** Pirates are not automatically evil, and Navy members are not automatically cruel. Individuals can act honorably inside a harmful system or selfishly beneath a noble banner.
- **Legacy and chosen purpose:** Caleb inherits an unfinished mission from his parents, but his journey should ultimately become his own choice rather than an obligation to copy them.

## Caleb's Goal and Early-Story Perspective

Caleb's stated goal is to become **the greatest Cosmoid Captain on the seas**. He wants to sail farther than anyone else, befriend powerful Cosmoids, and prove that he has what it takes to lead them as true partners. This dream should drive his early decisions, confidence, and eagerness to help others.

In early goal-focused dialogue, especially Caleb's Level 2 departure, frame his immediate motivation around this dream. Do not bring up Caleb's parents or the Isle of Whispers in the Level 2 introduction; reserve those plot threads for scenes specifically intended to advance the central mystery.

## Early Crew and Rivals

### Kip

Kip is a kind, observant, and timid young artist who begins the story as Captain Vane's unwilling cabin boy. His nervousness comes from living under Vane's ridicule and control, but his attention to small details lets him recognize qualities in Cosmoids that stronger or louder characters overlook. Mopup is his trusted Cosmoid partner.

Kip's dream is to travel every sea and **draw every kind of Cosmoid in the world**. After Caleb defeats Vane, Kip stays with Caleb as the crew's artist and Cosmoid chronicler. He is not the crew's navigator. His illustrated field guide should connect his dream to player progression: capturing or discovering Cosmoids gives him new creatures to study, draw, and describe.

Kip's decisive early character moment is publicly stating his dream in front of Vane, the person who taught him to feel ashamed of it. Caleb can give him the courage to speak, but Kip must make the choice himself.

### Mari and Tigrevel

Mari helps Caleb and Kip free Tigrevel from captivity beneath Ironbell in Level 5. After the Navy officer confrontation in Level 6, both Mari and Tigrevel join the sailing crew. Mari is the crew's **navigator**; demonstrate her practical navigation knowledge as part of her joining. Kip remains the artist and Cosmoid chronicler.

Tigrevel travels with them voluntarily but continues to refuse capture. Its missing trainer remains an unresolved mystery. Mari joining supersedes the earlier direction in which she stayed at Ironbell.

The enlarged crew agrees that it needs a bigger boat, establishing the immediate next objective after Ironbell.

### Ironbell Navy Officer

The officer who arrives at the end of Level 5 confronts the children in Level 6. His broad creative touchstones are Morgan's fixation on obedience and status and Lt. Surge's intimidating military confidence, expressed through an original Cosmoids character. He treats compassion as weakness and uses force to maintain control.

The officer throws a steel net over Tigrevel as Navy equipment, then sends **[Voltusk](docs/cosmoids/voltusk.md), his Steel/Electric walrus Cosmoid**, and **Electric-type minions** to drag Tigrevel back into captivity. The net is separate from the creature's anatomy. Voltusk stores electricity in its steel tusks, with larger tusks holding more charge, and uses sensitive metallic whiskers to find where lightning will strike. The minions' detailed mechanics remain undecided. At approximately half boss health, a paused intermission shows the officer forcing a Cursed Relic onto his partner to make it stronger. This triggers a painful Cursed Aura state, not a permanent Cursed evolution.

After victory, Caleb removes the forced relic and the creature's aura begins to subside, although it remains exhausted and needs care. This is not an established universal cure. The encounter plants the idea that cursed Cosmoids need rescuing too, without yet giving Caleb a full cure quest or revealing the curse's source. The immediate departure objective remains finding a bigger boat. See [the Level 6 direction](docs/levels/level-6.md) for encounter and ending details.

### Captain Vane

Captain Vane is a woman and the commanding captain of the pirate crew camped on the Level 2 island. She is polished, imposing, proud, and accustomed to treating obedience as proof of her superiority. Her teal, purple, white, and gold uniform, sculpted dark hair, elevated posture, and controlled expressions give her the bearing of someone who expects to dominate a room before a fight begins.

Vane mocks Kip's art and impossible-sounding dream because belittling her subordinates helps preserve her authority. When challenged, her amusement turns to anger and then disbelief; she cannot imagine that Caleb or Kip deserves to defy her.

In Level 3, Vane battles Caleb personally rather than sending her full crew against him. She considers him beneath the need for reinforcements and wants to reassert her control in front of Kip. The emotional structure should center on Kip overcoming his fear, declaring his dream, and choosing to leave with Caleb. The scene may echo the emotional clarity of an early pirate-adventure recruitment encounter, but its dialogue, creature battle, character roles, and resolution should remain specific to Cosmoids.

## The World

The game takes place during the pirate-and-Cosmoid era, across an enormous ocean filled with varied islands, cultures, habitats, ruins, and trade routes.

Cosmoids are strange creatures with elemental powers and unusual abilities. They live in the sea, on land, and in the skies. Some live in the wild, while others live and work alongside people. They help with island life, travel, sailing, labor, and production. Not everyone uses Cosmoids, trusts them, or considers them friendly.

Cosmoids can live inside magical doubloons made from different metals and materials, including gold and silver. Doubloon varieties can have different cultural, practical, or gameplay significance.

## Major Factions

### Pirates

Pirates are a mixed group rather than a single moral faction. Some are honorable adventurers pursuing freedom, discovery, and impossible dreams. Others are ruthless tyrants who raid islands, exploit the Stone Curse, and value profit or power over human and Cosmoid lives.

### The Navy

The Navy represents the established order of the seas. Many ordinary sailors sincerely want to protect people from pirates and the curse. Its higher leadership, however, is corrupt, controlling, and rigid. Rather than risk losing control while searching for a cure, leaders prefer to mercilessly quarantine or destroy infected islands.

Navy characters should reflect this tension. Some obey, some question orders, and some use the crisis to justify cruelty.

## The Stone Curse

The Stone Curse is a terrifying and poorly understood plague spreading across the islands. Most people know only rumors, and public fear often moves faster than reliable information.

### Effect on Humans

If an infected Cosmoid or cursed object scratches a human, the curse enters the victim's bloodstream. The victim slowly and painfully petrifies, eventually turning completely to stone. This is secretly happening to Captain Lance.

### Effect on Cosmoids

Cosmoids do not turn to stone. Instead, the curse crystallizes around their hearts and causes immense pain. A suffering Cosmoid becomes feral and is surrounded by a dark purple **Cursed Aura**. This state makes it much more aggressive, fast, and powerful in battle.

The creature is a victim, even when it is acting as a dangerous enemy. Story scenes should preserve that distinction.

### Cursed Relics

The curse behaves more like magical radiation than a conventional virus. Over centuries, sunken ships, treasure chests, ancient weapons, and other objects on the ocean floor have absorbed its aura.

Greedy pirate crews dredge up these **Cursed Relics** and force tamed Cosmoids to hold or wear them. This artificially triggers the Cursed Aura and grants a major power boost, while the pirates ignore the creatures' suffering.

### Cursed Evolutions

Exposure to cursed items can permanently alter a Cosmoid's growth. For some species, leveling up while holding a Cursed Relic causes the creature to absorb the aura and follow a dark **Cursed evolution** path.

This split should carry narrative weight. A Cursed evolution is not just a cosmetic variant or uncomplicated upgrade; it is connected to pain, exploitation, choice, and the possibility of healing.

## The Source: Patient Zero

The phenomenon's deeper name is the **Abyssal Resonance**. Its true source is a massive, ancient, tragic legendary Boss Cosmoid slumbering at the far end of the ocean on the Isle of Whispers. Its heavy aura leaks into the ocean currents and spreads through the world.

Caleb's explorer parents set sail to find the Isle of Whispers not for treasure, but to find a cure. Their missing expedition, Captain Lance's concealed illness, and the legendary Cosmoid form the central mystery connecting the island arcs.

Revelations about Patient Zero should emphasize tragedy and mystery before villainy. The final legendary Cosmoid is the source of the danger, but it should not feel like a generically evil monster.

## Story Structure Guidelines

Each island should feel like a satisfying short adventure while advancing at least one part of the larger journey. An island arc can include:

1. A memorable arrival, local custom, or visual identity.
2. A character or Cosmoid who gives the island's problem a personal face.
3. A conflict involving a local threat, pirate crew, Navy force, curse outbreak, or competing dream.
4. Battles and captures that arise naturally from the conflict.
5. A resolution that changes a relationship, frees someone, reveals a clue, or complicates what Caleb believes.
6. A reason to sail onward.

Not every island needs all six beats, and not every conflict should involve the Stone Curse. Smaller comic, competitive, or character-driven arcs keep the voyage varied and prevent the central mystery from overwhelming the adventure.

## Character Art Direction

Human characters should use a classic creature-adventure anime design language inspired by Ken Sugimori's work, with expressive silhouettes, clear shapes, and a thick, clean outline. Designs should suit the playful pirate setting and remain readable at game scale.

For Cosmoid-specific rendering and asset requirements, follow the creature art direction in `AGENTS.md`.

## Dialogue Rules

Dialogue should keep players emotionally invested without drowning them in text.

### Brevity and Pacing

- Keep individual dialogue lines short enough to read comfortably in the game panel.
- Give each line one main idea, joke, reaction, or emotional beat.
- Prefer a quick exchange between characters over one character delivering a long speech.
- Split necessary explanations across action, reactions, battles, and later scenes instead of delivering the entire answer at once.
- Avoid repeating information the player just saw or already learned.
- Start scenes late, once something interesting is happening, and end them once the scene's purpose has landed.
- Keep an ordinary story introduction or story part within **five to ten player-advanced dialogue panels or sequence entries total**. Five is a concise target for a simple beat; ten is the enforced ordinary maximum. This range applies equally to brainstorming drafts written in chat and dialogue implemented in the game.
- Count narration or action text as a panel when the player must advance through it. Do not evade the cap by splitting one beat across several short panels.
- Exceed ten panels only for a major turning point that genuinely needs more room. When proposing an exception, identify it explicitly and keep the scene as short as that turning point allows.
- Before presenting or implementing an ordinary introduction, count its panels and revise it into the five-to-ten range. Move secondary explanation, jokes, and worldbuilding into exploration, gameplay, or a later scene.

### Character and Emotion

- Every important scene should reveal at least one of the following: a character's motivation, flaw, relationship, fear, choice, or change.
- Let flaws create friction and consequences. Do not explain a flaw when a character can demonstrate it through a bad assumption, impulsive choice, defensive joke, or argument.
- Give characters distinct voices based on their outlook, background, confidence, and relationship to the listener.
- Use humor to strengthen character and relieve tension, not to erase sincere emotional moments.
- Allow reactions and interruptions. Characters should not sound as though they are taking turns reading lore from a script.
- Keep conflicts understandable for all ages without making every disagreement simplistic.

### Exposition and Worldbuilding

- Introduce lore when it matters to a decision or immediate problem.
- Prefer concrete details over encyclopedic explanations.
- Let different factions give incomplete, biased, or conflicting accounts of pirates, the Navy, Cosmoids, and the curse.
- Preserve mystery. Early characters should not speak with certainty about Patient Zero or the complete mechanics of the Abyssal Resonance.
- Use narration for brief transitions or actions that portraits cannot communicate, not for long prose passages.

### Dialogue Review Checklist

Before shipping a scene, confirm that:

- The scene has a clear purpose and changes something.
- The player can identify what each major character wants.
- At least one character trait is shown through behavior rather than explained.
- No speech is longer than it needs to be.
- Lore appears because it matters in the moment.
- The scene leaves room for gameplay, discovery, and visual storytelling.
- The tone balances adventurous fun with the scene's emotional stakes.
- Any branching line IDs and jumps follow the technical dialogue conventions in `PROJECT_NOTES.md`.
